Small wind turbines, micro hydro turbines and pedal generators usually use 3-phase permanent
magnet synchronous generators (PMSG). The rotor carries permanent magnets, and the stator has
three windings, typically connected in star (Y). This page describes a simple model of such a
generator in combination with a rectifier. The same model is used in the calculators of this
documentation.

## Poles and frequency

The magnets on the rotor alternate between north and south poles. The number of poles $p$ is
always even, and $p/2$ is the number of pole pairs. With every revolution, each winding sees
$p/2$ full periods of the magnetic field. The electrical frequency of the generator voltage is
therefore

$$
f_e = \frac{p}{2} \cdot n = \frac{p \cdot n}{2 \cdot 60\ \text{s/min}}
$$

with the rotational speed $n$, usually given in rpm (min⁻¹). For example, a generator with 16 poles
running at 600 rpm produces a voltage with

$$
f_e = \frac{16 \cdot 600\ \text{min}^{-1}}{2 \cdot 60\ \text{s/min}} = 80\ \text{Hz}
$$

The board measures the frequency of the generator voltage to determine the speed. This is why
the pole count is an important parameter: with a wrong value, all calculated speeds are off by
the same factor.

## Voltage

The voltage induced in the windings (back-EMF) is proportional to the speed. It is described by
the voltage constant $k_e$. In this documentation, $k_e$ is defined as the open-circuit RMS
voltage between two terminals (line-to-line) per speed, given in V/krpm (volts per 1000 rpm):

$$
V_{LL} = k_e \cdot n
$$

For example, a generator with $k_e = 70\ \text{V/krpm}$ running at 600 rpm produces

$$
V_{LL} = 70\ \text{V/krpm} \cdot 0.6\ \text{krpm} = 42\ \text{V}
$$

This definition is used because it can be measured directly with a multimeter at the generator
terminals, no matter whether the windings are connected in star or delta.

The rectifier converts the three AC voltages to DC. Without load, the rectified voltage equals
the average of the highest line-to-line voltage at any time:

$$
V_{dc,0} = \frac{3\sqrt{2}}{\pi} \, V_{LL} \approx 1.35 \, V_{LL}
$$

## Generator under load

When current flows, the voltage at the rectifier output drops below the open-circuit value. For
a 6-pulse rectifier, two phases conduct at any time. With the phase resistance $R$ and phase
inductance $L$ (star equivalent), the rectified voltage at DC current $I$ is approximately

$$
V_{dc} = V_{dc,0} - 2 R \, I - \frac{3}{\pi} \, \omega_e L \, I - V_{rect}
$$

with the electrical angular frequency $\omega_e = 2\pi f_e$ and the voltage drop $V_{rect}$ of
the rectifier.

- The term $2 R I$ is caused by the resistance of the windings. It results in **copper losses**
  of $P_{Cu} = 2 R I^2$, which heat the generator.
- The term $\frac{3}{\pi} \omega_e L I$ is caused by the inductance of the windings, which delays
  the transfer of the current from one phase to the next (commutation). This voltage drop is
  **not a loss**: the power is simply not drawn from the generator. However, it limits the power
  that can be extracted at a given speed.

The inductive voltage drop grows with the electrical frequency. Generators with many poles run at
high frequencies even at low speeds, so their inductance plays a larger role.

The power drawn from the generator shaft (ignoring friction and iron losses) is

$$
P_{shaft} = \left(V_{dc,0} - \frac{3}{\pi} \, \omega_e L \, I\right) \cdot I
$$

and the electrical power at the rectifier output is $P_{dc} = V_{dc} \cdot I$.

## Measuring your generator

If the parameters of a generator are unknown, they can be determined with a few simple
measurements. You need:

- A way to drive the generator at a constant speed, e.g. a drill or lathe, or the turbine itself
  in steady wind
- A tachometer (an optical tachometer with a reflective tape works well) or a known speed
- A multimeter that can measure AC voltage and frequency (true RMS)
- Optional: a milliohm meter or a lab power supply for the resistance, and an LCR meter for the
  inductance

<Callout title="Safety">
  Disconnect the generator from any load or electronics during the measurements. Never short the
  terminals of a spinning generator. Be careful with rotating parts.
</Callout>

**Open-circuit test:** Drive the generator at a constant speed without load. Measure the speed,
the AC voltage between two terminals and its frequency. Choose a speed in the normal operating
range of the generator.

**Resistance:** With the generator at rest, measure the resistance between two terminals. As the
values are usually well below 1 Ω, the resistance of the test leads matters. Use a 4-wire
measurement, or feed a known DC current (e.g. 1 A from a lab power supply) and measure the
voltage directly at the terminals.

**Inductance:** Measure the inductance between two terminals with an LCR meter. The value may
change with the rotor position, so take the average of a few positions. If the inductance is
unknown, the calculator can be used with a value of zero, but the results at high speeds will be
too optimistic.
