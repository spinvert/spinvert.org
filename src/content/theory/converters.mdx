After rectification, the DC voltage of the generator has to be matched to the battery voltage.
This page compares the options.

## Direct battery connection

The simplest system connects the rectifier directly to the battery. Current only flows once the
rectified generator voltage exceeds the battery voltage. In this case, the battery clamps the
voltage, and the current is determined by the difference between generator voltage and battery
voltage and by the internal resistance of the generator:

$$
I = \frac{V_{dc,0} - V_{bat}}{R_{int}}
$$

This has two drawbacks:

- Below the speed where the generator voltage reaches the battery voltage (cut-in speed), no
  power is harvested, even though it is available.
- The operating point is determined by the battery voltage and the generator, not by the needs
  of the turbine. The turbine therefore usually doesn't run at its
  [optimum](/theory/optimal-operation/), except by chance at a wind speed where the power drawn
  by the battery happens to match the optimal power.

For wind turbines, the generator is usually designed such that the cut-in speed is reached at a
low wind speed. At high wind speeds, the rotor is slowed down below its optimal speed, as the
battery keeps the generator output voltage low.

## Buck converter

A buck converter (step-down) produces an output voltage below its input voltage. With a buck
converter, the generator voltage has to stay _above_ the battery voltage at all relevant speeds.
The generator therefore has to be designed for a high voltage at low speeds, so its voltage
becomes very high at high speeds. Without load, e.g. if the battery is disconnected, the
open-circuit voltage of a wind turbine can reach several hundred volts.

Buck converters are common for solar charge controllers, where the open-circuit voltage of the
panels is limited and well known. For generators, the high voltages require more expensive
semiconductors and safety measures.

## Boost converter

A boost converter (step-up) produces an output voltage above its input voltage. The generator
voltage stays _below_ the battery voltage over most of the speed range, and all voltages can stay
in the extra-low voltage range.

A switch (usually a MOSFET) connects an inductor to ground, so that its current rises. When the
switch turns off, the inductor drives its current through a diode into the output at the higher
voltage. The
ratio between on-time and switching period, the duty cycle $D$, sets the voltage ratio:

$$
D = 1 - \frac{V_{in}}{V_{out}}
$$

By adjusting the duty cycle, the converter controls the current drawn from the generator. The
inductor has to carry this current, which is highest at low input voltages ($I_{in} = P / V_{in}$),
so a boost converter for high power at low voltage needs a large inductor.

A boost converter can't operate if the input voltage exceeds the output voltage. If this can
happen, e.g. at high speeds of a generator, the converter needs a bypass or has to be combined
with other measures to limit the voltage.

## Buck-boost converter

A buck-boost converter combines both functions and can produce an output voltage above or below
its input voltage. A common topology for this power range is the 4-switch buck-boost converter,
which consists of a buck half-bridge at the input and a boost half-bridge at the output, sharing
one inductor.

It allows a wide input voltage range and full control of the operating point at all speeds. In
exchange, it needs twice the number of switches, and the inductor has to be designed for the
highest current at the lowest input voltage.

## Comparison

| Option            | Harvests below battery voltage | Controls operating point | Effort |
| ----------------- | ------------------------------ | ------------------------ | ------ |
| Direct connection | No                             | No                       | Lowest |
| Buck converter    | No (generator must be above)   | Yes                      | Medium |
| Boost converter   | Yes                            | Below battery voltage    | Medium |
| Buck-boost        | Yes                            | Yes                      | High   |
| Boost with bypass | Yes                            | Below battery voltage    | Medium |

The last option is a compromise used by the [Spinvert Boost 4820](/hardware/boost-4820/design-decisions/#boost-stage-with-bypass):
a boost converter for the frequent low-power conditions, and a direct connection for the rare
high-power conditions.
