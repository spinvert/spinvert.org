The power curve of a wind turbine system alone is misleading when comparing systems. Most of the
time, the wind blows at low speeds, where the power is small but differences between systems
(e.g. the cut-in speed) are large. To get the energy over a year, the power curve has to be
weighted with the distribution of the wind speeds at the site:

$$
E = 8760\ \text{h} \cdot \int_0^\infty P(v) \, f(v) \, dv
$$

## Wind speed distribution

The distribution $f(v)$ is usually described by a Weibull distribution with a shape factor $k$
and a scale factor $c$:

$$
f(v) = \frac{k}{c} \left(\frac{v}{c}\right)^{k-1} e^{-(v/c)^k}
\quad \text{with} \quad
c = \frac{\bar{v}}{\Gamma(1 + 1/k)}
$$

For many sites, $k \approx 2$ is a good approximation (Rayleigh distribution). The distribution
is then fully defined by the annual mean wind speed $\bar{v}$. Values of $k$ above 2 describe
steadier winds, e.g. trade winds, values below 2 more variable winds.

## Typical sites

The mean wind speed is very site-specific. Small wind turbines are typically mounted at 10 to
20 m height, where obstacles such as buildings and trees reduce the wind speed considerably
compared to the values often quoted for large turbines at 100 m. As a rough guide:

| Site                       | Mean wind speed at 10 to 20 m |
| -------------------------- | ----------------------------- |
| Sheltered inland           | 3 to 4 m/s                    |
| Open inland terrain        | 4 to 5 m/s                    |
| Coastal                    | 5 to 6 m/s                    |
| Exposed coast or hilltop   | 6 to 8 m/s                    |

For a specific location, the [Global Wind Atlas](https://globalwindatlas.info/) provides mean
wind speeds and Weibull parameters, starting at 10 m height. Local obstacles are not included, so
measuring at the site is the most reliable option.

The [operating curve calculator](/hardware/boost-4820/operating-curve/#calculator) for the
Spinvert Boost 4820 uses this method to estimate the annual energy for a given site.
