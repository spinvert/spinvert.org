A wind turbine delivers its maximum power only at one particular speed for a given wind speed.
If the generator draws too little current, the rotor spins too fast. If it draws too much, the
rotor slows down and may even stall. This page derives the optimal operating curve and explains
how it can be tracked by the power electronics.

## Power in the wind

The kinetic power of the air flowing through the area $A = \pi R^2$ swept by a rotor with radius
$R$ at wind speed $v$ is

$$
P_{wind} = \frac{1}{2} \rho A v^3
$$

with the air density $\rho \approx 1.225\ \text{kg/m}^3$ at sea level. Because of the cube, doubling
the wind speed results in eight times the power.

A rotor can only extract a part of this power, described by the power coefficient $C_p$:

$$
P_{rotor} = C_p \cdot P_{wind}
$$

The theoretical maximum is the Betz limit of $C_p = 16/27 \approx 0.593$. Good small turbines
reach $C_{p,max} \approx 0.35$ to $0.45$.

## Tip speed ratio

The power coefficient depends on the ratio between the speed of the blade tips and the wind
speed, the tip speed ratio:

$$
\lambda = \frac{\omega R}{v}
$$

with the angular speed $\omega$ of the rotor in rad/s. Each rotor design has an optimal tip
speed ratio $\lambda_{opt}$ at which $C_p$ reaches its maximum. For 3-bladed rotors, it is
typically between 5 and 8. If the rotor turns faster or slower, $C_p$ drops. Above a certain
speed (runaway speed), the rotor isn't driven by the wind anymore.

## The optimal power curve

To extract the maximum power, the rotor speed must follow the wind speed such that
$\lambda = \lambda_{opt}$. Inserting $v = \omega R / \lambda_{opt}$ into the rotor power results in

$$
P_{opt} = \frac{1}{2} \rho \pi R^2 \, C_{p,max} \left(\frac{\omega R}{\lambda_{opt}}\right)^3
= \underbrace{\frac{\rho \pi R^5 \, C_{p,max}}{2 \lambda_{opt}^3}}_{k_{opt}} \cdot \omega^3
$$

The wind speed has disappeared from the equation. The optimal power only depends on the rotor
speed, with a constant $k_{opt}$ given by the rotor. If the generator is loaded with exactly
$P_{opt}(\omega)$, the rotor settles at its optimal speed for any wind speed:

- If the wind speed increases, the rotor power exceeds $P_{opt}$, so the rotor accelerates until
  it reaches the new optimal speed.
- If the wind speed decreases, the generator load exceeds the rotor power, so the rotor slows
  down accordingly.

This control principle doesn't need a wind speed sensor, only the rotor speed. The speed can be
derived from the frequency of the generator voltage.

## From power to generator current

Power electronics usually control the current drawn from the generator, so the optimal power
curve has to be translated into a current. Using the [generator model](/theory/generators/#generator-under-load),
the shaft power at DC current $I$ is

$$
P_{shaft} = \left(V_{dc,0} - X_c I\right) I
\quad \text{with} \quad
X_c = \frac{3}{\pi} \, \omega_e L
$$

Setting $P_{shaft} = P_{opt}$ and solving for the current results in

$$
I_{opt} = \frac{V_{dc,0} - \sqrt{V_{dc,0}^2 - 4 X_c P_{opt}}}{2 X_c}
$$

Both $V_{dc,0}$ and $X_c$ are proportional to the speed, so $I_{opt}$ can be calculated for
every speed and stored as a lookup curve over the electrical frequency. If the term under the
square root becomes negative, the generator can't deliver the optimal power at this speed because
of its inductance.

## Tracking methods

There are two common approaches to keep a wind turbine at its optimum.

**Perturb and observe:** Solar charge controllers usually find the maximum power point by
changing the operating point a little and checking whether the power increased. This doesn't work
well for wind turbines:

- The rotor has a large inertia. After a change of the generator current, the measured power is
  dominated by the rotor accelerating or slowing down, not by the aerodynamic power.
- The wind speed changes continuously, so the controller can't tell whether a power change was
  caused by its own action or by a gust.

**Lookup curve:** If the rotor characteristics are known, the optimal current can be calculated
for every speed as described above and stored as a curve. The controller measures the speed and
sets the current accordingly. This is fast and robust, and the closed loop is stable without any
knowledge of the wind speed: if the rotor is faster than optimal, the current is higher than the
rotor can sustain, so it slows down, and vice versa. The rotor inertia acts as a natural filter
for gusts. The drawback is that the curve has to be determined for each combination of rotor and
generator.

Measuring the speed via the generator frequency has an important advantage over using the
voltage: the voltage depends on the current because of the voltage drops in the generator, while
the frequency is an exact measure of the speed.

## Limits of the power electronics

In practice, the optimal curve can't be followed at all wind speeds:

- **Current or power limit:** If the optimal power exceeds the rating of the converter, the
  current has to be limited. The rotor then accelerates beyond its optimal speed until the rotor
  power matches the limit, which also increases the generator voltage.
- **Direct connection:** If the converter can't operate in some speed range, e.g. a boost
  converter with a generator voltage above the battery voltage, the battery voltage determines
  the operating point, as described in
  [DC/DC converters](/theory/converters/#direct-battery-connection).
- **No load:** If the battery is full or disconnected, the turbine has to be loaded by a dump
  load or braked. Otherwise, the rotor accelerates to its runaway speed, typically 1.5 to 2 times
  the optimal speed, and the generator voltage rises proportionally.
