A wind turbine delivers its maximum power only at one particular speed for a given wind speed.
If the generator draws too little current, the rotor spins too fast. If it draws too much, the
rotor slows down and may even stall. This page explains how the Spinvert Boost keeps the turbine
at its optimum and how the lookup curve for the firmware is calculated.

## Power in the wind

The kinetic power of the air flowing through the area $A = \pi R^2$ swept by a rotor with radius
$R$ at wind speed $v$ is

$$
P_{wind} = \frac{1}{2} \rho A v^3
$$

with the air density $\rho \approx 1.225\ \text{kg/m}^3$ at sea level. Because of the cube, doubling
the wind speed results in eight times the power.

A rotor can only extract a part of this power, described by the power coefficient $C_p$:

$$
P_{rotor} = C_p \cdot P_{wind}
$$

The theoretical maximum is the Betz limit of $C_p = 16/27 \approx 0.593$. Good small turbines
reach $C_{p,max} \approx 0.35$ to $0.45$.

## Tip speed ratio

The power coefficient depends on the ratio between the speed of the blade tips and the wind
speed, the tip speed ratio:

$$
\lambda = \frac{\omega R}{v}
$$

with the angular speed $\omega$ of the rotor in rad/s. Each rotor design has an optimal tip
speed ratio $\lambda_{opt}$ at which $C_p$ reaches its maximum. For 3-bladed rotors, it is
typically between 5 and 8. If the rotor turns faster or slower, $C_p$ drops. Above a certain
speed (runaway speed), the rotor isn't driven by the wind anymore.

## The optimal power curve

To extract the maximum power, the rotor speed must follow the wind speed such that
$\lambda = \lambda_{opt}$. Inserting $v = \omega R / \lambda_{opt}$ into the rotor power results in

$$
P_{opt} = \frac{1}{2} \rho \pi R^2 \, C_{p,max} \left(\frac{\omega R}{\lambda_{opt}}\right)^3
= \underbrace{\frac{\rho \pi R^5 \, C_{p,max}}{2 \lambda_{opt}^3}}_{k_{opt}} \cdot \omega^3
$$

The wind speed has disappeared from the equation. The optimal power only depends on the rotor
speed, with a constant $k_{opt}$ given by the rotor. If the generator is loaded with exactly
$P_{opt}(\omega)$, the rotor settles at its optimal speed for any wind speed:

- If the wind speed increases, the rotor power exceeds $P_{opt}$, so the rotor accelerates until
  it reaches the new optimal speed.
- If the wind speed decreases, the generator load exceeds the rotor power, so the rotor slows
  down accordingly.

This is the operating principle of the Spinvert Boost. It doesn't need a wind speed sensor, only
the rotor speed, which is derived from the generator frequency.

## From power to generator current

The firmware controls the input current of the boost converter, so the optimal power curve has
to be translated into a current. Using the [generator model](/theory/generators/#generator-under-load),
the shaft power at DC current $I$ is

$$
P_{shaft} = \left(V_{dc,0} - X_c I\right) I
\quad \text{with} \quad
X_c = \frac{3}{\pi} \, \omega_e L
$$

Setting $P_{shaft} = P_{opt}$ and solving for the current results in

$$
I_{opt} = \frac{V_{dc,0} - \sqrt{V_{dc,0}^2 - 4 X_c P_{opt}}}{2 X_c}
$$

Both $V_{dc,0}$ and $X_c$ are proportional to the speed, so $I_{opt}$ can be calculated for
every speed and stored as a lookup curve over the electrical frequency. If the term under the
square root becomes negative, the generator can't deliver the optimal power at this speed because
of its inductance.

## Limits of the board

The optimal curve can't be followed at all wind speeds:

- **Boost power limit:** The boost converter delivers up to 10 A at the battery voltage, i.e.
  about 480 W at 48 V. If the optimal power is higher, the current is limited. The rotor then
  accelerates beyond its optimal speed until the rotor power matches the limit.
- **Bypass:** Once the rectified voltage reaches the battery voltage, the boost converter can't
  operate anymore and the board switches to bypass. The generator is now connected directly to
  the battery, and the current is determined by the difference between generator voltage and
  battery voltage:
  $$
  I = \frac{V_{dc,0} - V_{bat}}{2R + X_c + 2R_{DS(on)}}
  $$
  The board can't control the operating point in this mode. Above 20 A, the ratings of bypass
  and rectifier are exceeded and the turbine has to be slowed down by other means.

## Choosing a generator

The voltage constant $k_e$ of the generator determines at which wind speed the board switches
from boost to bypass:

- With a **high** $k_e$, the rectified voltage reaches the battery voltage at low wind speeds.
  The board operates in bypass mode most of the time, and the benefit of the boost converter is
  small. In the extreme case, the behavior is the same as a direct battery connection.
- With a **low** $k_e$, the generator needs higher currents for the same power, which increases
  the copper losses. The boost power limit is reached before the bypass is possible, and the
  rotor runs faster than optimal in a wide range of wind speeds.

A good match is reached if the bypass starts at about the same wind speed as the boost power
limit, and the load line in bypass mode stays close to the optimal curve. Also check that the
open-circuit voltage at the maximum expected speed doesn't exceed the input voltage rating of
60 V, or make sure that the turbine is always loaded.

## Energy yield

The power curve alone is misleading when comparing systems. Most of the time, the wind blows at
low speeds, where the power is small but the boost stage makes the largest difference. To get the
energy over a year, the power curve has to be weighted with the distribution of the wind speeds at
the site:

$$
E = 8760\ \text{h} \cdot \int_0^\infty P(v) \, f(v) \, dv
$$

The distribution $f(v)$ is usually described by a Weibull distribution with a shape factor $k$
and a scale factor $c$:

$$
f(v) = \frac{k}{c} \left(\frac{v}{c}\right)^{k-1} e^{-(v/c)^k}
\quad \text{with} \quad
c = \frac{\bar{v}}{\Gamma(1 + 1/k)}
$$

For many sites, $k \approx 2$ is a good approximation (Rayleigh distribution). The distribution
is then fully defined by the annual mean wind speed $\bar{v}$. Values of $k$ above 2 describe
steadier winds, e.g. trade winds, values below 2 more variable winds.

The mean wind speed is very site-specific. Small wind turbines are typically mounted at 10 to
20 m height, where obstacles such as buildings and trees reduce the wind speed considerably
compared to the values often quoted for large turbines at 100 m. As a rough guide:

| Site                       | Mean wind speed at 10 to 20 m |
| -------------------------- | ----------------------------- |
| Sheltered inland           | 3 to 4 m/s                    |
| Open inland terrain        | 4 to 5 m/s                    |
| Coastal                    | 5 to 6 m/s                    |
| Exposed coast or hilltop   | 6 to 8 m/s                    |

For a specific location, the [Global Wind Atlas](https://globalwindatlas.info/) provides mean
wind speeds and Weibull parameters, starting at 10 m height. Local obstacles are not included, so
measuring at the site is the most reliable option.

### Comparison with a system without boost stage

The calculator compares the Spinvert Boost with the same board without boost stage, i.e. the
active rectifier always connected directly to the battery. With the same generator, the
comparison favors the Spinvert Boost, because a generator for a system without boost stage would
be designed for a higher voltage. The calculator therefore also determines the best rewound
version of the generator for this case: same magnets and copper volume, but a different number of
turns, so that resistance and inductance scale with $k_e^2$.

For the example turbine, the Spinvert Boost yields about 18 % more energy than the system with
the optimized generator at a sheltered site with 3.5 m/s mean wind speed. At 6.5 m/s, the
advantage drops to about 6 %. Compared to the same generator without boost stage, the gain is
much larger at low wind speeds. The boost stage pays off most at sites with low mean wind speeds
and when the generator can't be chosen freely.

## Other applications

The concept of a lookup curve over speed is not limited to wind turbines. Only the source of the
curve is different.

**Micro hydro:** The power available from water is $P = \rho g Q H \eta$ with flow rate $Q$,
head $H$ and turbine efficiency $\eta$. Impulse turbines such as Pelton wheels reach their
maximum efficiency when the runner speed is about half the jet velocity
$v_{jet} = \sqrt{2 g H}$. As the head is usually constant, the optimal speed is almost constant,
too. The lookup curve becomes very steep around the optimal speed, so that the turbine is held
at this speed independent of the flow rate.

**Pedal power:** Here, the curve defines how pedaling feels. A power curve rising with the cube of the
speed (like the one of a wind turbine) feels similar to riding a bicycle on a flat road, where
the air drag dominates. Cyclists
are most efficient at cadences between 60 and 90 rpm, so the gear ratio and the curve should be
chosen such that the desired power is reached in this range.
