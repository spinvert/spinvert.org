A battery needs DC, but a permanent magnet generator produces 3-phase AC with a voltage and
frequency proportional to its speed. The first stage of the power electronics is therefore a
rectifier. This page compares the common options.

## Diode bridge

The simplest rectifier is a 3-phase bridge with six diodes, two per phase. At any time, the
diode connected to the most positive phase and the diode connected to the most negative phase
conduct. The output is the highest line-to-line voltage, with a ripple of six pulses per period.
Without load, its average value is $V_{dc,0} \approx 1.35 \, V_{LL}$ (see
[Generators](/theory/generators/#voltage)).

Diode bridges are cheap, robust and need no control. Their drawback is the forward voltage
$V_F$ of the diodes. Two diodes are always in the current path, so the losses at DC current $I$
are

$$
P_{diode} = 2 \, V_F \, I
$$

With silicon diodes, $V_F$ is about 0.7 to 1 V, with Schottky diodes about 0.4 to 0.6 V at
high currents. A drop of 1 to 2 V is negligible for a high-voltage system, but not for small
generators: at 12 V, it corresponds to a loss of 10 % or more. The losses also have to be
dissipated as heat. At 20 A, a diode bridge dissipates around 30 W.

## Active rectifier

An active rectifier (also called synchronous rectifier) uses MOSFETs instead of diodes. Each
MOSFET is switched on during the interval in which the corresponding diode would conduct, so
the current flows through the channel resistance $R_{DS(on)}$ instead of a diode junction. The
body diodes of the MOSFETs take over during the short transitions.

The losses are approximately

$$
P_{active} = 2 \, R_{DS(on)} \, I^2
$$

With modern low-voltage MOSFETs, $R_{DS(on)}$ is only a few milliohms. The diode losses grow
linearly with the current, the MOSFET losses with its square, so the advantage is largest at low
and medium currents. For a given power, the current is inversely proportional to the voltage. At
low voltages, i.e. at low speeds, the current is relatively high and diode losses take a large
share of the power.

The price for the lower losses is a more complex circuit: the MOSFETs need gate drivers, and the
switching instants have to be derived from the phase voltages. Switching at the wrong time would
short-circuit two phases.

## Controlled rectifier

A 3-phase MOSFET bridge has the same structure as the inverter of a motor drive. Instead of only
mimicking diodes, it can be operated with pulse width modulation (PWM) to control the phase
currents directly. This enables some additional features:

- The phase currents can be sinusoidal and in phase with the induced voltage. This avoids the
  commutation voltage drop of a diode bridge and reduces torque ripple and acoustic noise.
- Together with the generator inductance, the bridge can work as a boost converter and raise the
  DC voltage above the rectified generator voltage, without an additional inductor.
- The generator can be operated as a motor, e.g. to start a vertical axis wind turbine.

On the other hand, the control needs the rotor position (from sensors or estimated from the
voltages and currents), and the switching losses and the effort for the control are considerably
higher. For small generators, a simple active rectifier followed by a separate DC/DC converter is
often the better compromise.
