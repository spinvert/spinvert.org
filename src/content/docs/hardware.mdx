The board consists of an active rectifier, a DC/DC boost converter with bypass, a dump load
output and the measurement and control circuitry. The schematic and PCB are designed with KiCad,
and the design files are available in the [GitHub repository](https://github.com/spinvert/).

```
                  +--------------+     +-----------------+
3-phase   ------->|    Active    |--+->|  DC/DC boost    |--+------> DC output
generator ------->| rectification|  |  |  (10 A @ 48 V)  |  |        (48 V)
          ------->|   (20 A)     |  |  +-----------------+  |
                  +--------------+  |                       |
                                    +---- Bypass (20 A) ----+
                                    |
                                    +---> Dump load (20 A)
```

## Active rectifier

The rectifier is a 3-phase bridge with six MOSFETs, two per phase. Instead of letting diodes
decide when current flows, the MOSFETs are switched on while the corresponding diode would
conduct, so that the current flows through the low channel resistance instead of a diode.

The losses of a diode bridge and an active rectifier at DC current $I$ are approximately

$$
P_{diode} = 2 \, V_F \, I
\qquad
P_{active} = 2 \, R_{DS(on)} \, I^2
$$

with the diode forward voltage $V_F$ and the on-resistance $R_{DS(on)}$ of the MOSFETs. Two
devices are always in the current path. The diode losses grow linearly with the current, while
the MOSFET losses grow with its square, so the advantage is largest at low and medium currents.

For a given power, the current is inversely proportional to the voltage. At low rectified
voltages, i.e. at low wind speeds, the current is relatively high and the diode losses take a
large share of the power. Compare both options:

## Boost converter

The boost converter steps the rectified voltage up to the battery voltage. A MOSFET (low-side
switch) connects the inductor to ground and builds up a current. When it turns off, the inductor
current flows to the output through a second MOSFET (high-side switch, synchronous rectifier).

In continuous conduction mode (CCM), the duty cycle $D$ of the low-side switch only depends on
the voltages:

$$
D = 1 - \frac{V_{in}}{V_{out}}
$$

During the on-time, the inductor current rises with a slope of $V_{in}/L$. The peak-to-peak
ripple of the inductor current is

$$
\Delta I_L = \frac{V_{in} \cdot D}{L \cdot f_{sw}}
$$

With $L = 47\ \mu\text{H}$ and $f_{sw} = 128\ \text{kHz}$, the ripple reaches its maximum of
about 2 A at $V_{in} = V_{out}/2$. If the average input current is lower than half the ripple,
the inductor current drops to zero in every period, and the converter operates in discontinuous
conduction mode (DCM). In DCM, the duty cycle depends on the load.

The boost stage is rated for 10 A output current. With $V_{out} = 48$ V, this is about 480 W.
At low input voltages, the input current $I_{in} = P / V_{in}$ becomes high, so the power is also
limited by the current rating at the input.

The high switching frequency allows a small inductor, which is available as an off-the-shelf
component. The input and output of the converter are filtered to meet EMC requirements.

## Bypass

When the rectified voltage reaches the battery voltage, the duty cycle of the boost converter
approaches zero and boosting is no longer possible. In this case, the bypass connects the
rectifier output directly to the battery. The bypass is rated for 20 A and has only conduction
losses.

In bypass mode, the battery voltage determines the rectified voltage. The current is then given
by the generator voltage, the battery voltage and the internal resistance of the generator, as
described in [Optimal operation](/docs/optimal-operation/#limits-of-the-board).

## Dump load

The dump load output is connected to the rectified voltage, in parallel to the boost converter
and the bypass. It can carry up to 20 A. Any resistive load can be used, e.g. heating elements
for water or air.

The resistance of the dump load determines how much power it can absorb at a given voltage:

$$
I = \frac{V_{dc}}{R_{dump}}
\qquad
P = \frac{V_{dc}^2}{R_{dump}}
$$

The resistance should be chosen such that the current stays below 20 A at the maximum input
voltage of 60 V, i.e. $R_{dump} \geq 3\ \Omega$. A 3 Ω load absorbs 768 W at 48 V and 1200 W
at 60 V, so its power rating must be sufficient for continuous operation.

## Measurements

The control of the board is based on the following measurements:

- **AC voltages** of the generator phases, used to determine the electrical frequency and thus
  the speed of the generator
- **Rectified voltage** at the input of the boost converter
- **Boost converter input current**, controlled according to the lookup curve

The speed is calculated from the frequency as $n = 120 \, f_e / p$, which requires the pole count
$p$ of the generator to be configured.

## Interfaces

- **USB** for monitoring and debugging
- **CAN-FD** for integration with other devices, e.g. battery management systems
