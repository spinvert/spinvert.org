The firmware of the Spinvert Boost controls the input current of the boost converter according
to a lookup curve over the generator frequency. The curve follows the optimal power curve of the
wind turbine, as derived in [Optimal operation](/theory/optimal-operation/). This page describes
how the ratings of the board affect the operation and contains a calculator for the curve.

## Limits of the board

The general limits are described in
[Optimal operation](/theory/optimal-operation/#limits-of-the-power-electronics). For the Spinvert
Boost 4820, the optimal curve can't be followed in the following cases:

- **Boost power limit:** The boost converter delivers up to 10 A at the battery voltage, i.e.
  about 480 W at 48 V. If the optimal power is higher, the current is limited. The rotor then
  accelerates beyond its optimal speed until the rotor power matches the limit.
- **Bypass:** Once the rectified voltage reaches the battery voltage, the boost converter can't
  operate anymore and the board switches to bypass. The generator is now connected directly to
  the battery, and the current is determined by the difference between generator voltage and
  battery voltage:
  $$
  I = \frac{V_{dc,0} - V_{bat}}{2R + X_c + 2R_{DS(on)}}
  $$
  The board can't control the operating point in this mode. Above 20 A, the ratings of bypass
  and rectifier are exceeded and the turbine has to be slowed down by other means.

## Choosing a generator

The voltage constant $k_e$ of the generator determines at which wind speed the board switches
from boost to bypass:

- With a **high** $k_e$, the rectified voltage reaches the battery voltage at low wind speeds.
  The board operates in bypass mode most of the time, and the benefit of the boost converter is
  small. In the extreme case, the behavior is the same as a direct battery connection.
- With a **low** $k_e$, the generator needs higher currents for the same power, which increases
  the copper losses. The boost power limit is reached before the bypass is possible, and the
  rotor runs faster than optimal in a wide range of wind speeds.

A good match is reached if the bypass starts at about the same wind speed as the boost power
limit, and the load line in bypass mode stays close to the optimal curve. Also check that the
open-circuit voltage at the maximum expected speed doesn't exceed the input voltage rating of
60 V, or make sure that the turbine is always loaded.

## Benefit of the boost stage

The calculator estimates the annual energy for a site, as described in
[Energy yield](/theory/energy-yield/). It compares the Spinvert Boost with the same board without
boost stage, i.e. the active rectifier always connected directly to the battery. With the same
generator, the
comparison favors the Spinvert Boost, because a generator for a system without boost stage would
be designed for a higher voltage. The calculator therefore also determines the best rewound
version of the generator for this case: same magnets and copper volume, but a different number of
turns, so that resistance and inductance scale with $k_e^2$.

For the example turbine, the Spinvert Boost yields about 18 % more energy than the system with
the optimized generator at a sheltered site with 3.5 m/s mean wind speed. At 6.5 m/s, the
advantage drops to about 6 %. Compared to the same generator without boost stage, the gain is
much larger at low wind speeds. The boost stage pays off most at sites with low mean wind speeds
and when the generator can't be chosen freely.

## Calculator

The calculator below determines the optimal operating curve for a wind turbine and simulates the
resulting operating points with the Spinvert Boost. For comparison, it also shows the same board
without boost stage, where the active rectifier is always connected directly to the battery.

If you don't know the parameters of your generator, measure them as described in
[Generators](/theory/generators/#measuring-your-generator). The rotor parameters $C_{p,max}$
and $\lambda_{opt}$ can often be found in the data sheet of the turbine or blades. If not, values
of 0.35 and 6 are a reasonable starting point for 3-bladed rotors.
