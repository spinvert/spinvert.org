This page explains the main design decisions behind the Spinvert Boost 4820 and the trade-offs
that were made. The general background can be found in the [Theory](/theory/) section.

## Boost converter with 48 V output

The Spinvert Boost uses a boost converter instead of a buck converter (see
[DC/DC converters](/theory/converters/) for a comparison). The generator voltage stays below the
battery voltage over most of the speed range, and power can be harvested even at low wind speeds,
where a directly connected generator would produce nothing. All voltages stay in the extra-low
voltage range below 60 V DC, which makes the system safer to install and allows cheaper
semiconductors with low on-resistance.

A 48 V battery or DC bus keeps the currents lower than with 12 V or 24 V systems and is the
standard for off-grid inverters in the power range of small wind turbines.

## Boost stage with bypass

The boost converter is rated for 10 A at 48 V (about 480 W). Above that, a bypass connects the
rectifier directly to the output and allows up to 20 A (about 960 W). This hybrid approach was
chosen for several reasons:

- **Cost and availability:** A DC/DC converter for 20 A would be considerably more expensive.
  Inductors for this current are not available as off-the-shelf components anymore. A 10 A stage
  including its EMC filtering can be built at low cost, while the bypass still allows higher
  currents when there is lots of wind.
- **Physics:** A boost converter can only increase the voltage. As soon as the rectified voltage
  reaches the battery voltage, the converter can't operate anymore, and a direct connection is the
  only option anyway.
- **Efficiency:** In bypass mode, only the conduction losses of the bypass switch occur. There are
  no switching losses and no inductor losses.
- **Wind statistics:** The power in the wind grows with the cube of the wind speed. At typical
  sites, most of the operating hours are at low wind speeds where the boost converter matters
  most, while full power is reached only rarely (see [Energy yield](/theory/energy-yield/)). It
  makes sense to size the converter for the frequent conditions.

The trade-off: in bypass mode, the battery voltage determines the operating point of the
turbine, and the board cannot control the speed anymore. The generator should be selected such
that the operating point in bypass mode stays close to the optimum. The
[calculator](/hardware/boost-4820/operating-curve/#calculator) shows how well a given generator
matches.

## Active rectification

The boost converter allows harvesting power at low rectified voltages, e.g. 12 V. At these
voltages, the drop of two diodes in a diode bridge would cause a loss of 10 % or more. The
Spinvert Boost therefore uses an active rectifier with MOSFETs, which has much lower losses and
produces less heat (see [Rectifiers](/theory/rectifiers/)).

## Dump load behind the rectifier

A wind turbine must never be left without load. Without load, the rotor accelerates to its
runaway speed, which is typically 1.5 to 2 times the speed at optimal operation. The mechanical
stress increases, and the generator voltage rises proportionally to the speed and can exceed the
input voltage rating of the board.

The dump load is connected directly behind the rectifier, in parallel to the boost converter
and the bypass. This way, it can load the generator independent of the converter stage and of
the battery: if the battery is full or disconnected, the dump load takes over. As it is connected
to the rectified DC voltage, a simple resistive load such as a water or air heater can be used.

The dump load can be driven with PWM. This way, exactly the power that can't be used otherwise
is diverted, and the turbine can stay at its optimal operating point even when the battery is
full. See [Dump load](/theory/dump-load/) for a comparison of the connection and control options.

The dump load is not sufficient to protect the turbine at high wind speeds. Its power is limited
by the ratings of the board, and it fails if the board fails. The turbine therefore needs a
secondary protection that works independently of the Spinvert Boost, e.g. furling or a separate
automatic electrical brake.

## Lookup curve instead of perturb and observe

Perturb and observe, the usual method of solar charge controllers, doesn't work well with the
inertia of a wind rotor and gusty wind (see
[Optimal operation](/theory/optimal-operation/#tracking-methods)). The Spinvert Boost instead
measures the generator speed and sets the current according to a lookup curve, which is
calculated from the known characteristics of turbine and generator. This is fast, robust and
doesn't need a wind speed sensor.

The speed is derived from the frequency of the generator voltage, which requires knowing the
number of poles of the generator.

## Based on Libre Solar

The hardware is based on the Libre Solar MPPT charge controllers, and the firmware will be the
generic Libre Solar charge controller firmware. This way, proven circuit blocks and features such
as battery charging algorithms, data logging and the ThingSet communication protocol can be
reused.

The CAN-FD interface allows integration into larger systems, e.g. with battery management systems
or other Libre Solar devices. The USB interface is used for monitoring and debugging.
