This page explains the main design decisions behind the Spinvert Boost and the trade-offs that
were made.

## Boost converter instead of buck converter

A permanent magnet generator produces a voltage proportional to its speed. Without a converter,
a battery can only be charged when the rectified generator voltage exceeds the battery voltage.
At low wind speeds, the turbine spins too slowly to reach this voltage. It produces nothing, even
though power is available in the wind.

A DC/DC converter decouples the generator voltage from the battery voltage. There are two
options:

- A **buck converter** (step-down) requires a generator voltage _above_ the battery voltage at
  all relevant speeds. The generator has to be designed for low speeds, so its voltage becomes
  very high at high speeds. Without load (e.g. if the battery is disconnected), the open-circuit
  voltage can reach several hundred volts.
- A **boost converter** (step-up) works with a generator voltage _below_ the battery voltage.
  The generator is designed so that it reaches the battery voltage only at high wind speeds.

The Spinvert Boost uses the boost approach. All voltages stay in the extra-low voltage range
below 60 V DC, which makes the system safer to install and allows cheaper semiconductors with low
on-resistance.

A 48 V battery or DC bus keeps the currents lower than with 12 V or 24 V systems and is the
standard for off-grid inverters in the power range of small wind turbines.

## Boost stage with bypass

The boost converter is rated for 10 A at 48 V (about 480 W). Above that, a bypass connects the
rectifier directly to the output and allows up to 20 A (about 960 W). This hybrid approach was
chosen for several reasons:

- **Cost and availability:** A DC/DC converter for 20 A would be considerably more expensive.
  Inductors for this current are not available as off-the-shelf components anymore. A 10 A stage
  including its EMC filtering can be built at low cost, while the bypass still allows higher
  currents when there is lots of wind.
- **Physics:** A boost converter can only increase the voltage. As soon as the rectified voltage
  reaches the battery voltage, the converter can't operate anymore, and a direct connection is the
  only option anyway.
- **Efficiency:** In bypass mode, only the conduction losses of the bypass switch occur. There are
  no switching losses and no inductor losses.
- **Wind statistics:** The power in the wind grows with the cube of the wind speed. At typical
  sites, most of the operating hours are at low wind speeds where the boost converter matters
  most, while full power is reached only rarely. It makes sense to size the converter for the
  frequent conditions.

The trade-off: in bypass mode, the battery voltage determines the operating point of the
turbine, and the board cannot control the speed anymore. The generator should be selected such
that the operating point in bypass mode stays close to the optimum. The
[calculator](/theory/optimal-operation/#calculator) shows how well a given generator matches.

## Active rectification

In a diode bridge, two diodes are always in the current path. Each causes a voltage drop of
about 0.5 to 1 V, so 1 to 2 V of the generator voltage are lost. At 48 V this is a loss of 2 to
4 %. However, the boost converter allows harvesting power at much lower voltages, e.g. 12 V,
where the same diode drop causes a loss of 10 % or more.

An active rectifier replaces the diodes by MOSFETs. Their losses depend on the on-resistance and
are much lower at low and medium currents. They also produce less heat: at 20 A, a diode bridge
dissipates around 30 W, while MOSFETs with 5 mΩ on-resistance dissipate only a few watts. See
[Hardware: Active rectifier](/hardware/boost-4820/circuit/#active-rectifier) for an interactive comparison.

## Dump load behind the rectifier

A wind turbine must never be left without load. Without load, the rotor accelerates to its
runaway speed, which is typically 1.5 to 2 times the speed at optimal operation. The mechanical
stress increases, and the generator voltage rises proportionally to the speed and can exceed the
input voltage rating of the board.

The dump load is connected directly behind the rectifier, in parallel to the boost converter
and the bypass. This way, it can load the generator independent of the converter stage and of
the battery: if the battery is full or disconnected, the dump load takes over. As it is connected
to the rectified DC voltage, a simple resistive load such as a water or air heater can be used.

## Lookup curve instead of perturb and observe

Solar charge controllers usually find the maximum power point by _perturb and observe_: they
change the operating point a little and check whether the power increased. This doesn't work well
for wind turbines:

- The rotor has a large inertia. After a change of the generator current, the measured power is
  dominated by the rotor accelerating or slowing down, not by the aerodynamic power.
- The wind speed changes continuously, so the controller can't tell whether a power change was
  caused by its own action or by a gust.

Instead, the Spinvert Boost uses the known characteristics of the turbine. At the optimum, the
power of a wind rotor is proportional to the cube of its speed. The board measures the speed and
sets the generator current according to a lookup curve. This is fast, robust and doesn't need a
wind speed sensor. The derivation and a calculator for the curve can be found in
[Optimal operation](/theory/optimal-operation/).

The speed is derived from the frequency of the generator voltage, which requires knowing the
number of poles of the generator. In contrast to the voltage, the frequency is not affected by
voltage drops in the generator windings and is therefore an exact measure of the speed.

## Based on Libre Solar

The hardware is based on the Libre Solar MPPT charge controllers, and the firmware will be the
generic Libre Solar charge controller firmware. This way, proven circuit blocks and features such
as battery charging algorithms, data logging and the ThingSet communication protocol can be
reused.

The CAN-FD interface allows integration into larger systems, e.g. with battery management systems
or other Libre Solar devices. The USB interface is used for monitoring and debugging.
